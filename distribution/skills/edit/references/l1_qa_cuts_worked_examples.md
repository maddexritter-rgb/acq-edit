# L1 Q&A cuts: worked editorial comparisons

These are paraphrased research examples, not dialogue to insert into a clip. Use the
actual source words. They illustrate selection decisions, not a fixed beat count or
certified frame boundaries. A polished reference can contain a fidelity mistake.

## Temporary slowdown: keep the obstacle and the accommodation

**Full exchange:** A service owner has continuing referrals, capped prices and about
15% operating profit. He keeps accepting clients while building systems reactively.
He worries that declining someone means failing to help them. The discussion covers
customer economics, a temporary intake slowdown, a ninety-day systems sprint, a waitlist,
a hypothetical margin increase, and an optional paid-priority variation.

**Useful short:** Establish the business constraint and operating margin. Keep that demand
already exists and the slowdown is temporary. Keep the empathy objection, followed by
the three-month waitlist and ninety-day sprint. End on the conditional operational upside.
Moving the margin question earlier can make the rate-cap discussion easier to understand.

**Why the omissions work:** The paid-priority variation is a separate extension; the
ordinary waitlist already answers the retained objection. Gross margin and detailed
per-customer arithmetic can go if the remaining operating-margin mechanism is intelligible.
Do not merge gross margin and operating profit into one metric.

**What would break it:** Removing the existing-demand condition makes the advice sound
universal. Removing the objection can make the accommodation feel unmotivated. Turning
an if-based margin hypothesis into a promise changes the source's certainty. A broad
closing claim in the reference is not evidence that capacity becomes unlimited.

## Hiring: retain both sides of the match

**Full exchange:** An operator lacks dedicated acquisition time. The discussion includes
team rosters, role confusion, profitable existing work, hiring cost, leadership concerns,
and recruiting a small agency owner into an established organic business.

**Useful short:** Retain the bottleneck and enough business economics to understand the
hire. Explain why the recruit can perform (acquisition and small-team experience) and
why the job could appeal (less agency stress, compensation, an established brand).
A later edit restoring salary context matters; an earlier draft is not the final policy.

**Tightening check:** If the accusation that current work is unproductive is removed,
its long defensive clarification may no longer be needed. If an answer between two
versions of a question is removed, do not accidentally keep both questions back-to-back.
A leadership concern can remain when the cut intentionally covers that tradeoff, but
do not imply that recruiting alone resolved it.

## Emotional stakes: a factual correction outranks a cleaner hook

**Full exchange:** An owner makes an emotionally charged claim about a family member,
then explicitly retracts that claim. The discussion turns to guilt and buying back time.

**Reference risk:** Keeping the original claim and a later guilt label while omitting
the explicit retraction leaves a stronger assertion than the corrected source supports.
The emotional label is not an equivalent factual correction.

**Faithful options:** Keep the correction with the assertion, or remove the incorrect
assertion and choose a source-grounded setup that still establishes the actual tradeoff.
Preserve the money-to-time reasoning and the owner's chosen criterion when they form
the payoff. Do not imitate a reference's omission merely because the reference looks polished.

## Expansion: distinguish diagnosis from applicability

**Full exchange:** A multi-location operator has strong margins, a membership ceiling,
reliable incoming customers, and an earlier successful expansion. The discussion calculates
the ceiling, then compares marginal improvement with opening another location.

**Useful compression:** If the reward is choosing where to focus, the long calculation can
give way to a concise diagnosis and comparison. If the reward is teaching the ceiling
calculation, the inputs and math are the content and should remain. Choose the short's
purpose before deciding the calculation is expendable.

**What must survive:** The facts that make expansion advice applicable. High margins do
not establish reliable demand. Keep a confirming reply with the question it answers. A
phrase such as “on this edge” cannot replace the diagnostic context it points back to.

**Numerical tradeoff:** A revenue comparison and a profit opportunity-cost comparison
use different units. Keep their basis intelligible, or remove the optional numerical
comparison as a unit. Hypothetical future results are not money already lost.

## Worked example: keep the application without importing its results

**Full exchange:** A studio owner wants a saleable business. The host tells a long
fitness-business example: compare service economics, simplify the offer, then pilot a
small staffing model. The example includes its own revenue, margins and employee count.
The host then explicitly returns to the owner: examine your profitable times and
packages, simplify operations, and build something another person can run.

**Useful compression:** Keep the owner's desired outcome, the reason the current path
may not achieve it, and the host's direct application. The worked example can go when
the selected promise is an operating-model direction rather than a lesson in calculating
service economics. Preserve the uncertainty of the recommended path.

**What would break it:** Turning the example's staffing count into a universal requirement,
or attaching its revenue and margins to the owner's prospective business. “Fewest people
who can run this” is a different claim from prescribing the example's exact team size.
If the source never makes the application, do not invent one merely because the two
businesses seem similar. Keep the comparison intelligible or choose another complete idea.
